Yes — very much so, but it's split across four literatures that barely cite each other. Here's the map, plus where your specific framing is actually novel.

First, reading your example back: a b a b b c c b b is 5/9 b — b is functioning as a refrain that everything returns to. That's rondo logic (ABACABA family) wearing pop clothes: verse–chorus–verse–chorus–chorus–bridge–bridge–chorus–chorus. Worth knowing because the interesting parameter isn't "which letters" but "how strong is the gravitational pull back to the refrain."

1. Music theory: form (descriptive, mature, ~200 years old)

Search terms: sectional form, formal function, Formenlehre. Canonical taxonomy — strophic (AAA), binary, rounded binary (AABA), ternary, rondo, 32-bar AABA, 12-bar blues, verse–chorus. For popular music specifically: John Covach's "Form in Rock Music: A Primer," Jay Summach's Form in Popular Song 1955–1989, Drew Nobile's Form as Harmony in Rock Music, de Clercq & Temperley's rock corpus work. This gives you a vocabulary and a set of attested archetypes, but it's descriptive — no generative model.

The theory-side paper closest to your instinct is Brad Osborn's "Subverting the Verse–Chorus Paradigm: Terminally Climactic Forms in Recent Rock Music" (Music Theory Spectrum 35/1, 2013) — forms organized around an energy trajectory toward a single late peak rather than around thematic recapitulation. That's the "dynamic change" axis you mentioned, treated formally.

2. MIR: structural segmentation + corpora (empirical — this is your data source)

This subfield produces literally the strings you're describing. Foote's self-similarity matrices (1999) → Paulus, Müller, Nieto & McFee (MSAF). More useful to you: the annotated datasets.

  • SALAMI — ~1400 tracks, human-annotated multi-level letter labels.
  • Harmonix Set — 912 pop songs, beat/downbeat/section annotations.
  • POP909 (arXiv:2008.07142) plus Dai, Zhang & Dannenberg's phrase analysis (arXiv:2010.07518) — the notation is nearly identical to yours: alternating letters and digits, letter = phrase identity, digit = length in bars, capital = melodic phrase, lowercase = non-melodic (so i4A8A8B8B8o4 = 4-bar intro, two 8-bar A's, two 8-bar B's, 4-bar outro). They report 50–90% of a typical song is covered by repeated phrases, and their extraction hits ~93% agreement with human labels.

Practical upshot: you can mine transition probabilities, length distributions, and repetition-run statistics from these directly, rather than inventing priors.

3. Computational generation (the actual "template generator" work)

Older: formal grammars (Steedman's jazz grammar; Lerdahl & Jackendoff's GTTM for hierarchy), Markov chains over section labels, L-systems. Newer, hierarchical: MusicVAE's hierarchical decoder, Pop Music Transformer, and most relevantly "Whole-Song Hierarchical Generation of Symbolic Music Using Cascaded Diffusion Models" (ICLR 2024) — explicitly generates form → phrase → chords → notes as a conditioning cascade. That's the modern answer to "generate the template first, fill it in second."

The one you should actually read for aibleton: TOMI (ISMIR 2025, arXiv:2506.23094) — LLM-driven multi-track electronic composition modeled as a sparse 4D space of clips × sections × tracks × transformations, integrated into REAPER. That's your architecture with a different DAW.

The gap that matters for you: nearly all of this treats a section as a melodic/harmonic identity. Your framing — a section is a rhythmic or dynamic delta — is arrangement/energy modeling, and it's much thinner in the literature. Osborn is the theory hook; TOMI's "transformations" axis is the closest computational one.

4. Jam / real-time (documented, essentially not formalized)

This is the "open form" tradition: Terry Riley's In C (modules + cueing rules), Stockhausen's Klavierstück XI, Earle Brown's mobile forms, John Zorn's Cobra (a game piece — a prompter and a card vocabulary, i.e. a live structure generator), Butch Morris's Conduction, Walter Thompson's Soundpainting. Fan-side, the Phish "Type I vs. Type II jam" distinction (stays inside the form vs. departs from it) is a real, widely-used taxonomy for exactly this.

Nobody has turned this into a computational model in a serious way. And it's a genuinely different object: a jam template isn't a fixed sequence, it's a state machine with cue conditions and exit conditions — "stay in b until energy plateaus, then either c or back to b." That's where your project would be doing something that isn't already sitting in a paper.

What I'd build, given that

Not a sequence sampler. Three layers:

  1. Grammar/state machine over section labels — production rules or a weighted transition matrix, with priors mined from SALAMI/POP909 rather than hand-tuned.
  2. An energy/tension curve as the conditioning signal — the template is sampled to fit a trajectory (arch, terminally-climactic, plateau), which is what makes "b is a dynamic lift, c is a breakdown" fall out naturally instead of being annotated by hand.
  3. Section = a bundle of deltas, not a chunk of notes: {density, register, subdivision, harmonic rhythm, instrumentation mask} relative to a reference. Reusable across a fixed harmonic bed, which is what jam-band form actually is.

For live use, layers 1 and 3 map cleanly onto tools already in your MCP surface: Session View scenes + follow actions for the state machine, arrangement locators for the committed template. I'd stop short of the neural layer until the grammar-plus-curve version is boring.

Read the code. Here's the stripped-down version — energy/tension stays as a field nobody reads yet, so a plugin can fill it in later without a migration.

Three simplifications

1. A template is a string. "a8 b8 a8 b8 b8 c4 c4 b8 b8" — letter = section, digit = bars. That's the POP909 convention from the last message. Storage is trivial, you can type one by hand, and generator output is reviewable by eye. parseForm / stringifyForm round-trip it.

2. A section is a text brief, not a feature vector. "driving four-on-the-floor, dry" is the Splice prompt. That's the whole payload. intensity?: number sits in the type unused — that's your plugin hook.

3. Letters and material are separate stages. Generating the form needs no data and no LLM (60 lines of seeded sampling). Binding letters to Splice stacks is a separate, cached step. So you can regenerate forms freely without spending credits.

Types — packages/protocol/src/templates.ts

export const SectionSchema = z.object({
  label: z.string().regex(/^[a-z]$/),
  bars: z.number().int().positive(),
  brief: z.string(),              // the Splice prompt
  intensity: z.number().min(0).max(1).optional(),  // reserved; nothing reads it yet
});

export const TemplateSchema = z.object({
  id: z.string(),
  name: z.string(),
  form: z.string(),               // "a8 b8 a8 b8 b8 c4 c4 b8 b8"
  sections: z.record(z.string(), SectionSchema),
  bpm: z.number().optional(),
  createdAt: z.number(),
});

/** A template bound to real Splice material. Persisted so re-rendering costs no credits. */
export const CompositionSchema = z.object({
  id: z.string(),
  templateId: z.string(),
  bpm: z.number(),
  stacks: z.record(z.string(), z.object({          // label -> resolved material
    stackUuid: z.string(),
    layers: z.array(z.object({
      layerType: z.string(),                        // drums | bass | keys | ...
      soundUuid: z.string(),
      localPath: z.string().nullable(),             // null until downloaded
    })),
  })),
  layout: z.array(z.object({ label: z.string(), startBeat: z.number(), lengthBeats: z.number() })),
});

Sections live inside the template rather than in a shared library. Extract later if you actually want reusable sections across songs.

Generator — pure, seeded, no data

generateForm({ seed, alphabet: 3, count: 9, home: "b", maxRun: 2 }): string

Rules that get you 90% of the way: start on a; introduce letters strictly in order (c can't appear before b); at most one new letter per step; weighted pull back toward home; cap consecutive repeats at maxRun. Deterministic from seed, so a form you like is a shareable integer.

Briefs default by role — a = groove, b = lift, c = contrast/breakdown — and the brain (or you) rewrites them. The LLM's value here is the briefs, not the letters. Don't spend a model call picking letters.

API split — this is the part that matters architecturally

Templates are CRUD; composing is a DAW mutation. Don't conflate them.

Route Why
REST GET/POST/DELETE /templates, GET /templates/:id Library data, no ports touched
REST POST /templates/generate Pure function, returns without persisting
Command { type: "compose", templateId, bpm?, download?: false } Touches Splice + Ableton, must be cancellable

The command goes through the mailbox so the existing machine owns acting/pause/cancel and emits action.applied. CRUD does not — ExternalCommand shouldn't grow a templateCreate variant.

Store: file-backed TemplateStore in mate/src/core/templates.ts, path from Config. Not in StateStore — that's the live DAW picture, this is a library.

Compose is one effect, not an action list

The pipeline is data-dependent (stack → layers → download → tracks → clips), which doesn't fit Decision.actions[]. Model it like callBrain: a new Effect in EffectRunner that runs a Composer against both ports and posts composeDone / composeFailed. EffectRunner stays the only impure part.

Render to Session View first. One audio track per Splice layer type, one scene per distinct label. Firing a scene = going to a section — that is the jam use case, and it's ~4 label-scenes regardless of how long the form is. "Commit to arrangement" is a separate action: walk layout, duplicateToArrangement at each startBeat, createLocator per entry.

Model — a band is the space axis to a template's time axis:

BandPart { id, role, name, brief, emphasis? }   // id is stable — a future
Band     { id, name, parts[], metadata, createdAt }   // Composition keys on it

That join is why parts get stable ids rather than just names. part.brief is the Splice prompt axis, exactly like section.brief; emphasis? is the reserved plugin hook, mirroring section.intensity.

Metadata is Record<string,string>, defaulted to {} and never required. genre is a conventional key, not a schema field — the generator takes a genre as input and writes it back to metadata.genre; the UI filters on metadata.genre ?? "—" so untagged bands are their own bucket.

Generation is a readable data table, not branching logic: per genre, core roles always present plus weighted optionals, with name and brief pools per role. Duplicate roles draw distinct names without replacement (two keys → Rhodes + clav, not "keys 1/keys 2"). Omitting the genre picks one from the seed, so an empty generate still returns a coherent band.

Yes — and it's possible because you insisted bands stay complete. That constraint is what makes it work.

The filter can't live on the band

Add muted or playedBy to a part and the same funk band can't serve a drummer and a bassist. The band is a library asset describing the ensemble; who's in the room is a different fact with a different lifetime. Nothing in BandPartSchema should change.

The seat

Seat { roles: string[]; partIds: string[] }

Two layers, because you need both:

  • roles — the standing preference. "I play drums" applies automatically to every band you ever open, including ones generated later.
  • partIds — the per-take override, for "this metal band has three guitars and I'm playing the lead."

Resolution is a pure helper in protocol, alongside bandRoles and bandGenre:

seatedParts(band, seat): { covered: BandPart[]; played: BandPart[] }

played is yours, covered is mate's. The band goes in whole and comes out partitioned — nothing is removed from it.

Where it lives — four touch points, all existing patterns

goal is already exactly this shape, so the seat mirrors it: Config gets MATE_SEAT_ROLES=drums (a startup default, which is what "drummers first" in your CLAUDE.md actually means) → seeds StateStore.seat → ExternalCommand.seatSet overrides at runtime → MateEvent.seat.changed pushes it to the UI.

roles persists via config because "I'm the drummer" shouldn't die on restart. partIds is session-only and should be — it's tied to the band you have open right now. No new document store, no migration.

What Ableton does with a played part

The track exists, is empty, and is record-armed — not omitted. Two reasons:

  1. Composition.material[part.id] is simply absent for that part, so the schema I already wrote doesn't change.
  2. Your take lands in the right lane, in band order, alongside mate's parts. That's the practice loop — mate plays the other seven, you fill the eighth, and it's captured.

Omitting the track entirely is the degenerate option, worth a flag, not the default.

UI

A per-part "I play this" toggle on the roster. The lane dims and marks itself as yours. It writes to the seat, never to the band — and you can prove that by diffing the band's JSON on disk before and after.

One consequence worth naming now

The seat is the first state the composer reads that is neither the template nor the band. So step 7's command should be { templateId, bandId } and read the seat from state — not take it as an argument. Otherwise every caller has to thread it through, and the standing "I'm a drummer" preference stops being standing.
